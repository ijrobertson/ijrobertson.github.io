Ser vs Estar in Spanish

📚 Explore Spanish: Spanish Basics First Conversation At the Restaurant At the Hotel At the Airport Typical Day Spanish Hub →
Spanish grammar guide · Updated October 2026 · 12 min read

The short answer

Ser describes what something is: its identity, characteristics, origin, profession, the time and date, and where an event takes place (Soy profesor — I’m a teacher). Estar describes how or where something is: location, physical and emotional states, conditions and actions in progress (Estoy cansado — I’m tired). To remember them, use DOCTOR for ser and PLACE for estar.

Ser and Estar at a Glance

SER Description Occupation Characteristic Time & date Origin Relationship + events
ESTAR Position Location Action (-ando / -iendo) Condition Emotion

English has one verb for “to be”. Spanish has two, and choosing between ser and estar is one of the first big hurdles every learner meets. You may have been told that ser is for permanent things and estar is for temporary ones. That rule gets you part of the way, but it breaks surprisingly often. This guide gives you a more reliable way to think about it, every use with real example sentences, the adjectives that change meaning depending on the verb, and a quiz to test yourself.

Both verbs are irregular, so it’s worth learning these forms by heart. Here are the three tenses you’ll use most.

💡 Tip: the preterite of ser (fui, fuiste, fue…) is identical to the preterite of ir (to go). Context tells you which one is meant: Fue un buen día (It was a good day) vs Fue al cine (He went to the cinema).

The Trick: DOCTOR and PLACE

The most popular way to remember the uses is two acronyms. If what you’re describing fits DOCTOR, use ser. If it fits PLACE, use estar.

DOCTOR → serPLACE → estar
DescriptionEs alta.PositionEstá sentado.
OccupationSoy médico.LocationEstoy en casa.
CharacteristicEres simpático.ActionEstoy comiendo.
TimeSon las tres.ConditionEstá roto.
OriginSoy de Chile.EmotionEstá triste.
RelationshipEs mi hermana.

Behind the acronyms is one simple idea: ser tells you what something is, while estar tells you the state or place it’s in.

When to Use Ser

Ser defines things. It answers “what is it?”, “who is it?” and “what is it like?”.

SER

1. Description

what someone or something is like
  • Mi hermana es alta.My sister is tall.
  • La casa es muy grande.The house is very big.
  • Sus ojos son verdes.Her eyes are green.
SER

2. Occupation

what someone does for a living
  • Soy profesor.I’m a teacher.
  • Mi madre es médica.My mother is a doctor.
  • Somos estudiantes.We’re students.
💡 Tip: Spanish drops the article before a profession: Soy profesor, not Soy un profesor. Add un/una only when you describe the person further: Es un profesor excelente.
SER

3. Characteristics

personality and defining traits
  • Juan es muy simpático.Juan is very nice.
  • Eres inteligente.You’re smart.
  • Mis vecinos son un poco raros.My neighbors are a bit strange.
SER

4. Time and dates

the hour, the day, the date
  • Son las tres y media.It’s half past three.
  • Es la una.It’s one o’clock.
  • Hoy es lunes.Today is Monday.
  • Es tarde.It’s late.
SER

5. Origin and material

where someone is from, what something is made of
  • Soy de Colombia.I’m from Colombia.
  • Ella es mexicana.She’s Mexican.
  • La mesa es de madera.The table is made of wood.
SER

6. Relationships

how people are connected
  • Ana es mi hermana.Ana is my sister.
  • Somos amigos desde hace años.We’ve been friends for years.
SER

7. Where an event takes place

the big exception to “location = estar”
  • La fiesta es en mi casa.The party is at my house.
  • ¿Dónde es el concierto?Where is the concert?
  • La boda fue en la playa.The wedding was on the beach.
SER

8. Impersonal expressions and the passive

“it is…” and “was done by”
  • Es importante practicar todos los días.It’s important to practice every day.
  • Es posible que llueva.It might rain.
  • El libro fue escrito por Isabel Allende.The book was written by Isabel Allende. (see por vs para)

When to Use Estar

Estar describes the state something is in or where it is. It answers “how is it?” and “where is it?”.

ESTAR

1. Position

physical posture
  • El profesor está sentado.The teacher is sitting down.
  • Estamos de pie.We’re standing.
  • Mi abuela está acostada.My grandmother is lying down.
ESTAR

2. Location

where people, places and things are
  • ¿Dónde está el baño?Where is the bathroom?
  • Madrid está en el centro de España.Madrid is in the center of Spain.
  • Estoy en casa.I’m at home.
📍 Remember: this applies even to things that never move. Cities, mountains and buildings all take estar: Lima está en Perú. Only events take ser.
ESTAR

3. Action in progress

estar + gerund (-ando / -iendo)
  • Estoy estudiando español.I’m studying Spanish.
  • ¿Qué estás haciendo?What are you doing?
  • Estaban comiendo cuando llegué.They were eating when I arrived.
ESTAR

4. Condition

physical state, or the result of a change
  • La sopa está fría.The soup is cold.
  • La tienda está cerrada.The shop is closed.
  • Mi teléfono está roto.My phone is broken.
  • Estoy enfermo.I’m sick.
ESTAR

5. Emotion

how someone feels
  • Estoy muy cansado.I’m very tired.
  • Mi hermana está nerviosa por el examen.My sister is nervous about the exam.
  • ¿Por qué estás enfadado?Why are you angry?
ESTAR

6. Common expressions

fixed phrases worth learning as vocabulary
estar de acuerdo — to agree
estar de vacaciones — to be on vacation
estar a punto de — to be about to
estar de buen / mal humor — to be in a good / bad mood
estar harto de — to be fed up with
estar embarazada — to be pregnant

Why “Permanent vs Temporary” Fails

Many textbooks teach that ser is for permanent things and estar is for temporary ones. It’s a handy first guess, but these everyday sentences break it:

Ser, but temporary
Es joven.He’s young. (won’t last forever)Son las cinco.It’s five o’clock. (only for a minute)Soy estudiante.I’m a student. (for a few years)
Estar, but permanent
Está muerto.He’s dead. (very permanent)Roma está en Italia.Rome is in Italy. (not moving)Está casada.She’s married. (often said with estar)

The idea that works every time: ser identifies or classifies (what something is, what kind of thing it is), and estar locates or reports a state (where it is, what condition it’s in, often as the result of a change). Death, marriage and being broken are all states that came about through a change, so they take estar.

Common Mistakes English Speakers Make

Soy cansado.Estoy cansado.Feelings and physical states take estar.
Estoy de Canadá.Soy de Canadá.Where you’re from is origin: ser.
El supermercado es en la esquina.El supermercado está en la esquina.The location of a place or thing takes estar.
La fiesta está en mi casa.La fiesta es en mi casa.Where an event takes place uses ser.
Soy un profesor.Soy profesor.No article before an unmodified profession.
Están las tres.Son las tres.Telling the time always uses ser.
Mi abuelo es muerto.Mi abuelo está muerto.Being dead is a state, so it takes estar (even though it’s permanent).

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between ser and estar?

Ser describes what something is: its identity, characteristics, origin, profession, the time and date, and where an event takes place (Soy profesor, Es lunes). Estar describes how or where something is: location, physical and emotional states, conditions and actions in progress (Estoy en casa, Estoy cansado, Estoy estudiando).

Is it “soy cansado” or “estoy cansado”?

Estoy cansado. Being tired is a state you are in, so it takes estar. Soy cansado isn’t used to mean “I am tired”; ser cansado describes something tiring, as in El viaje es cansado (The trip is tiring).

Why is it “está muerto” and not “es muerto”?

Being dead is the result of a change of state, and states take estar, even a permanent one. It’s the clearest proof that the permanent vs temporary rule doesn’t work: Está muerto uses estar, while Es joven uses ser.

Do you use ser or estar for location?

Use estar for where people, places and things are (Madrid está en España, ¿Dónde está el baño?). Use ser for where an event takes place (La fiesta es en mi casa, ¿Dónde es el concierto?).

Is it “soy feliz” or “estoy feliz”?

Both are correct. Soy feliz describes you as a happy person, or says you’re happy with your life in general. Estoy feliz says you feel happy right now, often because of something that happened.

Does ser mean permanent and estar mean temporary?

Not quite. It works as a rough guide, but it fails often: Es joven (young is temporary) uses ser, and Está muerto (dead is permanent) uses estar. A more reliable way to think about it: ser says what something is, and estar says what state or place it’s in. See why the rule fails.
